# Writing guide

The reader is a developer who wants to grow. Many read English as a second language. Write so they can read a lesson once, understand it, and act on it the same day.

## Voice

- **Point first.** Open each section with the idea in one plain sentence, then explain.
- **Short sentences.** Aim for under 20 words. One idea per sentence.
- **Plain words.** "Use", not "leverage". "Look into", not "delve into". Explain any term a junior wouldn't know the first time it appears.
- **Talk to the reader.** Use "you" and contractions ("you're", "don't"). Sound like a kind senior colleague, not a textbook.
- **No idioms that don't translate.** "Hit the ground running" is hard for many readers. Say what you mean.
- **No filler.** Skip "comprehensive", "robust", "seamless", "crucial", "in today's fast-paced world".
- **No em or en dashes.** Use a period, a comma, a colon or parentheses. A test enforces this.

## Every lesson has

1. A short opening: why this matters, ideally with a small real-world scene.
2. Two to five sections, each with a concrete example. Show, don't describe.
3. At least one `Compare` block (weak vs strong).
4. Two or three `Drill`s the reader can do this week, with a time estimate.
5. A `Takeaways` list of three to five lines.

Keep it to 900-1400 words, or 6-10 minutes.

## Examples

- Generic and realistic: a checkout, a signup form, a report, a cron job.
- Never real company, client or colleague names. Made-up first names are fine.
- Code samples are short (under 20 lines), correct, and have a language tag.

## Claims

- If you cite a number, link the source and say how old it is.
- Quotes need a public author. If you can't attribute it, rewrite it as your own advice.
- Prefer "this often happens" over invented precision.
