Imagine you spend three days building a perfect feature, and then someone says, "Oh, that's not what we meant." Almost every engineer has had this happen. It hurts, and it's mostly avoidable.

The fix is simple, but it takes discipline: **understand the problem before you start building the solution.** This lesson shows you how.

## Look at the real thing before you assume

Tickets are written by busy people. They're often short, a bit wrong, or written in terms of a solution ("add a button that...") instead of a problem ("users can't find...").

Before you write any code:

- **Reproduce it.** If it's a bug, see it happen yourself.
- **Look at the live system.** How does it work today? Which settings does it use? What do real users actually see?
- **Read the code that's there.** Find where the behavior comes from. Don't guess from the file names.

<Callout type="warn" title="Staging is not production">
A test copy of a system can have different settings, data or versions. If the question is "how does it work for users," check where users are.
</Callout>

## Define the problem, then the solution

Write the problem down before you think about how to fix it. A good way is the **two-sentence goal**:

1. **What** should change, and for whom.
2. **Why** it matters.

<Compare>
<Before label="Solution only">

"Add a loading spinner to the search page."

</Before>
<After label="Problem and why">

"Users think search is broken because nothing happens for two seconds after they type. We want them to see that results are coming, so they don't leave the page."

</After>
</Compare>

The second version opens better options. Maybe a spinner is right. Maybe search should just be faster. Maybe showing old results while new ones load is better. You can't see those options if you start from "add a spinner."

If you can't write the two sentences, you don't understand the task yet. That's fine. It just means your next step is to ask, not to code.

## Check whether a tool already solves it

Before you build something, spend ten minutes looking for what already exists:

- Does the codebase already have a helper for this? Search for it.
- Does your framework or platform have a built-in feature?
- Is there a well-known library that's used and maintained?

```bash
# Before writing a new date formatter, check for an existing one
git grep -nE "formatDate|toLocaleDateString" src/
```

A second copy of something that already exists is not just wasted time. Later the two copies drift apart and disagree, and then you have a bug.

## Watch out for the XY problem

The XY problem goes like this: someone wants to do **X**. They decide **Y** is the way to do it. Y is hard, so they ask for help with Y, and never mention X.

> "How do I get the last three characters of a file name?"
>
> (What they actually want: the file's type. And the file type isn't always three characters long, like `.jpeg` or `.md`.)

You'll see the XY problem in tickets, in questions from teammates, and in your own head. When something feels oddly hard, step back and ask: **what am I really trying to do?**

When others ask you for help, gently ask about X: "Sure. What are you trying to do in the end?" It often saves both of you an hour.

## Ask one specific question

Asking questions is not weakness. Asking **vague** questions too early, or asking nothing for two days, is the problem.

A good question comes after a short investigation and has three parts:

1. What you're trying to do.
2. What you found or tried.
3. The one specific thing you need.

<Compare>
<Before label="Hard to answer">

"Hey, do you have a minute? I have a question about the invoices."

</Before>
<After label="Easy to answer">

"I'm adding a tax line to invoices. The total comes from `calcTotal()`, but the PDF uses its own math in `renderInvoice()`. Should the PDF read the total from `calcTotal()` instead, or is there a reason it's separate?"

</After>
</Compare>

The second question can be answered in one line. It also shows you looked into it first.

Two more habits:

- **Don't ask to ask.** Skip "Can I ask a question?" Just ask it.
- **Ask the question in a shared place** when it's not private. Then the answer helps the next person too.

## A rough question at 15 minutes beats a perfect one at 5 pm

There's a balance. Don't ask before you've looked at all. But don't spend a whole day stuck either.

A simple rule many teams use: **if you've been stuck for about 15 to 30 minutes and you're not making progress, ask.** Your question can be rough. "I'm stuck on X, I've tried A and B, any ideas?" is fine.

The cost of asking is a few minutes of someone else's time. The cost of not asking can be days of yours.

<Callout type="tip" title="Write it down as you go">
Keep a scratch file while you investigate: what you checked, what you found, what you ruled out. When you ask for help, paste the useful part. When you write the PR, half of it is already written.
</Callout>

## Know when you're ready to start

You're ready to build when you can answer these questions:

- What's the goal, in two sentences?
- How will I know it worked? (What will I check, and where?)
- What's out of scope?
- Is there anything that already does part of this?

If one answer is missing, spend a few more minutes before you code. Those minutes are the cheapest part of the whole task.

<Drill id="build-two-sentence-goal" title="Rewrite three tickets as two-sentence goals" time="20 min">

Take three tickets or tasks from your current work or a side project. Rewrite each as two sentences: what changes and for whom, then why it matters. If you can't, write down the question you'd need to ask to find out.

</Drill>

<Drill id="build-existing-tool" title="Search before you build" time="15 min">

Next time you're about to write a helper or a component, stop. Spend ten minutes searching the codebase and the docs for something that already does it. Write down what you found, even if it's "nothing."

</Drill>

<Drill id="build-good-question" title="Ask one great question" time="10 min">

The next time you're stuck, write your question with three parts: what you want, what you tried, the one thing you need. Read it once and cut any word that doesn't help. Then send it in a shared channel.

</Drill>

<Takeaways>

- Look at the real system and read the real code before you assume anything.
- Write the goal in two sentences: what changes, and why.
- Search for an existing tool or helper before you build a new one.
- When something feels oddly hard, ask what you're really trying to do.
- Ask one specific question after a short investigation. Don't wait all day.

</Takeaways>
