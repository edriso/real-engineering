You've reached the last lesson. Reading this guide was the easy part. The engineers who stand out after five or ten years aren't the ones who read the most. They're the ones who kept practicing a little, every week, for a long time.

This lesson is about how to keep growing without burning out.

## Practice on purpose

Doing the same work every day doesn't make you better by itself. Ten years of experience can be one year repeated ten times. Growth comes from **deliberate practice**: picking one skill, practicing it just past your comfort zone, and getting feedback.

<Compare>
<Before label="Just doing">

"I write code every day, so I'm getting better."

</Before>
<After label="Practicing on purpose">

"This month I'm working on smaller pull requests. My goal is under 300 lines each. I check the size before I open every one, and I ask my reviewer if it was easier to review."

</After>
</Compare>

Three things make practice work:

1. **One skill at a time.** Pick a single thing, like writing tests first, or shorter messages.
2. **A clear target.** "Better at tests" is vague. "Every bug fix this month comes with a test that fails without the fix" is clear.
3. **Feedback.** From a person, a measurement or a tool. Without feedback, you're guessing.

## A weekly learning loop

You don't need hours a day. A small routine that you actually keep beats a big plan you drop after a week. Here's a simple loop that takes about two hours a week:

| When | What | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Pick this week's one skill to practice | 5 min |
| During the week | Use it in your real work | as you go |
| Twice a week | Read some good code or one good article | 30 min each |
| Friday | Write what you learned and what you'd change | 15 min |
| Once a month | Teach or share one thing you learned | 1 hour |

The [practice page](/practice) turns this into daily, weekly and monthly habits you can tick off.

## Read good code

Writers get better by reading. Engineers do too. Pick a well-known open source project in the language you use and read part of it. Don't try to understand everything. Ask:

- How is this folder organized? Why?
- How do they name things?
- How do they handle errors?
- What do their tests look like?
- What do their pull requests and commit messages look like?

You'll start to notice patterns you can borrow. You'll also see that good code is often plain and boring, and that's on purpose.

## Teach what you learn

The fastest way to find gaps in your understanding is to explain something to someone else. You'll notice right away which parts you can't explain clearly.

Teaching doesn't need a stage. You can:

- Write a short post about a bug you fixed.
- Explain a concept to a junior teammate or a friend.
- Answer a question in a forum or community chat.
- Write a clear README or setup guide for your team.

As a bonus, teaching is public proof of your skills. See [build proof in public](/lessons/build-proof-in-public).

## Keep a learning log

Memory is unreliable. Write things down. A plain text file is enough. Two kinds of notes help most:

**A learning log.** What you learned, in one or two lines, with a link.

```md
2026-11-10  Learned: a test that passes before AND after my fix proves nothing.
            Now I run new tests against the old code first.
2026-11-12  Learned: git bisect found a bad commit in 6 steps out of 80 commits.
```

**A decision log.** When you make a choice that wasn't obvious, write down what you chose, why, and what you didn't choose. Months later, it saves you from arguing the same decision again, and it shows how your judgment has changed.

## Your skill with AI keeps growing

AI tools will keep getting better on their own. You don't have to do anything for that. What doesn't improve on its own is your skill at using them: knowing what to ask, giving good context, checking the output and knowing when not to use AI at all.

That skill builds on itself. Every prompt you refine, every claim you check and every eval you write makes the next session better. Two people with the same tools can get very different results, and the difference keeps growing over time. See [work with AI agents](/lessons/work-with-ai-agents).

## Start before you're ready

<Quote by="G. K. Chesterton">
If a thing is worth doing, it is worth doing badly.
</Quote>

Your first blog post will be awkward. Your first open source pull request might get rejected. Your first talk will have pauses. That's the price of getting better, and everyone pays it. Waiting until you're "ready" usually means never starting.

Do it badly, then do it again a bit better.

## Ask for feedback

Feedback is the fastest way to grow, but most people don't get much of it unless they ask. The trick is to ask specific questions:

<Compare>
<Before label="Too general">

"Any feedback for me?"

</Before>
<After label="Easy to answer">

"In my last two pull requests, was the description clear enough to review without asking me questions? What would have made it faster?"

</After>
</Compare>

When you get feedback, say thank you, and don't argue in the moment. Think about it later. You don't have to agree with all of it, but look for patterns. If two people say the same thing, it's probably true.

## Go deep in one thing, wide in many

Companies value people who are very good at one thing and know enough about the things around it to work well with others. Some people call this being T-shaped.

- **Depth:** pick one area and go deep. Frontend performance, databases, testing, security, developer tools. This is what people will come to you for.
- **Breadth:** learn the basics of the areas next to yours. If you're frontend, learn enough about APIs, databases and deploys to have a useful conversation.

You don't have to choose your depth forever. Pick one for the next year and see where it leads.

## Calm, steady pace

<Callout type="note" title="Rest is part of the work">
Tired engineers ship bugs. Long nights feel productive but often create more work the next day. Sleep, exercise, time with your family and time away from screens aren't breaks from getting better. They're part of it.
</Callout>

Growth is a long game. Two hours of practice every week for five years adds up to more than 500 hours. That's more than most people ever put in on purpose. You don't need to rush. You need to keep going.

<Takeaways>

- Practice one skill at a time, with a clear target and feedback.
- A small weekly routine you keep beats a big plan you drop.
- Read good code, teach what you learn and write it down.
- Your skill at using AI grows over time, so keep refining it.
- Start before you're ready, and ask for specific feedback.
- Go deep in one area, wide in the rest, and keep a calm, steady pace.

</Takeaways>
